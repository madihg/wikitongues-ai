# Measure first, then build Igala speech

**Igala is not a blank slate for speech recognition, and it is a blank slate for speech synthesis.** Meta's Omnilingual ASR already lists Igala (`igl_Latn`), trained on about 7.6 h of Igala audio, with a reported **CER of 27.6 (Igala, 7B LLM-ASR, test set not stated)** ([per-language CSV](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/facebookresearch/omnilingual-asr/main/per_language_results_table_7B_llm_asr.csv)), so the first step is a free measurement on the 45-minute GRN recording, not a collection drive. No Igala TTS voice, no Igala entry in WAXAL, and no Igala among the 26 LINGUA Africa awardees turned up in the notes. The best-evidenced route for TTS is one studio-quality speaker recording **5 to 15 h** of tone-marked, community-written, domain-matched script, then fine-tuning a pretrained multilingual model (OmniVoice first, MMS-TTS or VITS as a non-commercial baseline). On Yoruba, OmniVoice fine-tuning cut CER from 5.6% to **2.7% at 5 h and 1.7% at 15 h** ([DunDun preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14817v1)). For ASR, seed an Omnilingual CTC model and build a **10 h** corpus from 10 or more speakers, transcribed tone-stripped, with a tone-marked tier on a subset. Evaluation should be a frozen, speaker-disjoint, privately held test set plus a blind pairwise arena with minimal-pair tone items, because ASR-based metrics hide tone errors and listener ratings favour fluent but wrong audio. Direct pay is cheap (about $16 per speaker-hour in Kenya), transcription is the cost driver (**about $460 to $1,370 per transcribed hour, my arithmetic**), and the plan fits inside a LINGUA-sized data tier (up to $50,000 cash). No funder has a live call; the realistic doors are Masakhane Hub grantees, the Lanfrica and NaijaVoices team, and the Gates coalition working groups. Every hours-to-quality number below comes from another language (Yoruba, Efik, Akan, Ewe, Kinyarwanda and others); no Igala experiment exists in the literature reviewed, and the report marks inference separately from cited findings.

## Igala has a 7.6-hour ASR baseline and no TTS, and the hooks are thin

The notes overturn one assumption: public Igala ASR does exist, in a limited form. Omnilingual ASR (Nov 2025, 1,600+ languages, Apache-2.0 code and models) includes `igl_Latn` in its language list ([lang_ids.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/facebookresearch/omnilingual-asr/main/src/omnilingual_asr/models/wav2vec2_llama/lang_ids.py), [paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.09690), [GitHub](https://github.com/facebookresearch/omnilingual-asr)). The per-language table gives Igala **7.6 h of training data and CER 27.6 (Igala, 7B LLM-ASR)**, against Idoma 7.3 h and CER 27.4 (Idoma), Nupe 8.4 h and CER 28.1 (Nupe), Esan 7.8 h and CER 55.4 (Esan), Yoruba 828.1 h and CER 9.0 (Yoruba), Igbo 729.6 h and CER 9.1 (Igbo), Hausa 836.4 h and CER 3.5 (Hausa) ([per-language CSV](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/facebookresearch/omnilingual-asr/main/per_language_results_table_7B_llm_asr.csv)). The CSV does not say which test set produced each CER or whether tone marks were scored. The Igala audio itself is not in Meta's public corpus: the `facebook/omnilingual-asr-corpus` has 348 language folders and no `igl_Latn` ([HF dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/facebook/omnilingual-asr-corpus)). The paper credits the Lanfrica and NaijaVoices work with new datasets for 11 African languages, Igala among them, totalling **110 h across those 11 languages** ([Omnilingual ASR HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09690)). Dividing gives about 10 h each (my arithmetic). Hours, speakers, licence and protocol for the Igala set are unpublished, and the Mozilla Data Collective listing of six NaijaVoices datasets has no Igala entry ([NaijaVoices org page](https://datacollective.mozillafoundation.org/organization/cmidd9v3l002nmh07kmij7fq5)).

**Inference.** The Lanfrica Igala set is probably the source of Meta's 7.6 h, but no source confirms it. That matters twice: it is the first partner to contact (it is either reusable training data or the benchmark you must beat), and if the GRN recording or similar religious audio sat in Meta's training mix, the 27.6 baseline would flatter the model. The notes cannot rule that out. Also correct the framing: Igala speech data exists, so "first Igala speech dataset" is wrong. The defensible novelty is an open, consented, benchmarked Igala corpus plus the first Igala TTS voice.

Everywhere else in the literature, Igala is absent. The WAXAL card lists Nigerian configs for Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba and Nigerian Pidgin only, all TTS ([WaxalNLP card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/google/WaxalNLP)). NaijaVoices covers about 600 h each of Hausa, Igbo and Yoruba ([NaijaVoices](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20564)). The LINGUA Africa Nigerian awardees cover Hausa, Yoruba and Igbo ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/academic-program/lingua-africa-open-call-for-inclusive-ai-language-projects/)). SimbaBench and AfriVoices-KE contain zero mentions of Igala by text search ([SimbaBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.18436), [AfriVoices-KE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.08448)). The one existing Igala audio is religious media from Global Recordings Network with no research transcripts ([GRN Igala](https://globalrecordings.net/en/language/igl)).

## One studio voice of 5 to 15 hours is the best-evidenced TTS bet

The direct evidence is Yoruba, plus one Efik data point. IroyinSpeech reports that a good-quality single-speaker Yoruba VITS voice is possible with **as little as 5 h (Yoruba, author claim, no listener score on the abstract page)** ([IroyinSpeech](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.16071)). The 2026 DunDun preprint fine-tuned OmniVoice on Yoruba budgets of 1, 5, 15 and 28.5 h and found CER falling from **5.6% to 2.7% at 5 h and 1.7% at 15 h (Yoruba)**, with tone saturating within the first hour (Yoruba) ([DunDun](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14817v1)). It is an unreviewed preprint. For Efik, a tonal Nigerian language, a 3 h single-speaker corpus gave MMS-TTS a **MOS of 3.80 +/- 0.63 (Efik)** when initialised from a Yoruba checkpoint with an extended vocabulary, and tonal errors persisted in every system tested ([Efik TTS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.04515)). Zero-shot cloning does not rescue an unseen language: OmniVoice zero-shot Yoruba tone score was 0.567 against a native anchor of 0.596 (Yoruba), but intelligibility needed fine-tuning ([DunDun](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14817v1)).

Model choice and licence decide what can be released openly.

The OpenBibleTTS ranking is the strongest cautionary fact in the TTS literature: listeners ranked Gemini-TTS best while ASR-based WER ranked EveryVoice best, and open from-scratch systems degrade sharply on out-of-domain text ([OpenBibleTTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09553v1)). Compute is modest: VITS took about 30 h on 2 L40S GPUs, EveryVoice about 25 h and F5-TTS about 68 h at 500,000 updates each (all trained on about 90 h per language) ([OpenBibleTTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09553v1)).

**Inference.** Fine-tune, do not train from scratch. EveryVoice's strong numbers come from about 90 h per language, so nothing shows it works at 5 h. A pretrained base (OmniVoice, or an MMS-TTS checkpoint if non-commercial release is acceptable) is the safer start, and a three-way arena between OmniVoice fine-tune, MMS-TTS and EveryVoice or VITS is the right way to choose. Licence order of operations: confirm the OmniVoice licence first, because a community open release rules out the non-commercial bases (MMS-TTS, F5-TTS) unless that is a deliberate choice. Record a recording script of roughly 45,000 words for 5 h and 135,000 for 15 h (150 words a minute, raw reading before editing losses; the WAXAL script was about 108,500 words per language, a comparable order ([WAXAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02734v3))). The script is the real bottleneck, and it must be domain-matched: Bible-only training degrades out of domain, and the text-side use case is Wikipedia-style prose, numbers and names. Copy the WAXAL TTS recipe: scripts drafted by community pairs, a studio-like box, one voice per actor, segmentation and review ([Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/waxal-a-large-scale-open-resource-for-african-language-speech-technology/)). WAXAL also states that its ASR data is not suited to single-speaker TTS ([WAXAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02734v3)), so TTS and ASR need separate sessions. The WAXAL sources disagree on hours and languages (the paper says about 235 h TTS over 13 languages, the blog over 565 h over 27), and none reports a TTS MOS, so WAXAL gives a process to copy, not a quality target.

## Tone marks: record them for TTS, strip them for the ASR headline

Igala writing has traditionally omitted tone since the 1930s, and the only sources found on its tone system are non-scholarly: a learner site claims five tones, and a linguistics article notes that downstepped high is realised near mid and often written as mid ([Ki-Gala](https://ki-gala.com/five-tones-of-igala-tongue), [Wikipedia Igala](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Igala_language), [Linguistik online](https://bop.unibe.ch/linguistik-online/article/download/3844/5824/13553)). No Igala orthography standard, tone-restoration tool or tone benchmark was found. Yoruba is the proxy, and it points in two opposite directions depending on the task.

For TTS, tone marks matter. A snippet of the IroyinSpeech abstract reports male-voice MOS of **3.98 with diacritics against 1.86 without (Yoruba, training-text comparison, snippet only)** ([IroyinSpeech](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.16071)). DunDun requires reliably diacritised input and warns that under-marked text turns High and Low targets into Mid, so a flattened voice looks good on unmarked text ([DunDun](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14817v1)). CER computed through an ASR that drops diacritics cannot see this: DunDun's tone metric collapses under pitch flattening while CER does not. No study measured synthesis from stripped versus marked text at inference time; the IroyinSpeech comparison is on training text and is snippet-only.

For ASR, tone marks hurt current models. On a small Yoruba Common Voice set (3,099 utterances), Whisper-small WER was **65.70% tone-marked against 37.90% unmarked (Yoruba)**, MMS-1B-all 85.08% against 50.27% (Yoruba), with the authors blaming tokenisation and weak tonal cues ([Olusanya, LoResLM 2026](https://aclanthology.org/2026.loreslm-1.14)). With about 95.6 h of Yoruba-English speech and a character-level Omnilingual 300M model the gap shrinks to **16.15% against 13.53% WER (Yoruba-English code-switching)** ([yecsASR-omni-llm](https://huggingface.co/LyngualLabs/yecsASR-omni-llm)). WAXAL-NET found tonal and non-tonal languages at near-identical macro WER of about 37 to 38% (19 African languages) and argued that how densely the orthography marks tone is the driver: Ikposo 75.3% (Ikposo, dense Latin diacritics) against Luganda 16.9% (Luganda, light marking) ([WAXAL-NET](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02375)).

Restoring marks with an LLM is plausible but untested for Igala. In the LREC 2026 study, off-the-shelf LLMs beat specialised diacritisation models on Yoruba and Arabic, small models hallucinated, and LoRA fine-tuning on a small Yoruba set helped ([Toyin et al.](https://aclanthology.org/2026.lrec-1.40/)). Older Yoruba systems reached **96.23% word-level accuracy (Yoruba, about 250,000 words)** ([Asahiah et al.](https://journals.agh.edu.pl/csci/article/view/2128)) and a diacritisation error rate below 5% (Yoruba) ([Orife 2018](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2018/orife18_interspeech.html)), yet a ByT5 restorer still shows a Yoruba CER of 0.11 partly because of genuine tonal ambiguity ([diacnet-1.0](https://huggingface.co/olaverse/diacnet-1.0)).

**Inference.** Three conventions follow. First, the TTS script and the studio speaker's transcript carry tone marks, written under one documented convention by the speaker and one or two careful annotators, since the training text sets the ceiling on tone accuracy. Second, ASR base transcripts are tone-stripped with under-dot and nasal letters kept, one agreed spelling for contractions and a written normalisation table, plus a tone-marked tier on a subset by the best annotators; this matches the practice in the notes and the Yoruba evidence above. Third, the Gemini 3.1 Pro step with retrieval becomes a tone-restoration front end for TTS, but it must be scored on a held-out tone-marked Igala set before it voices anything, and the TTS must be reported separately on gold-marked and LLM-restored input so preprocessing error is not charged to the voice. Igala has far less text than Yoruba, so expect lower restoration accuracy. Measure inter-annotator agreement on tone marks in the pilot; no published figure exists for any African language and inconsistent marking reads as noise to a model.

## ASR should start from Omnilingual and a 10-hour community corpus

The notes agree on a route ranking. Under 10 h, seed from a multilingual self-supervised model: in the Omnilingual paper's fine-tuning study of 11 languages with 5 to 10 h each, CTC fine-tuning at lr 1e-5 from an OmniASR CTC checkpoint (5K steps for 300M and 1B) beat the 7B LLM-ASR for most languages, often reaching CER under 5; one Nigerian comparator with 7.6 h stayed at CER 29.7 ([Omnilingual ASR PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.09690)). The CTC 300M fine-tune took about 1 h on 32 GPUs, roughly 32 GPU-hours. In the 13-language African benchmark, w2v-BERT 2.0 and MMS-1B were the most data-efficient at 1 to 10 h, Whisper never won at 1 h, and XLS-R and MMS can fail outright at 1 h (WER 100% for MMS on Ewe) ([benchmark](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.10968v1)). The tonal curves flatten: Akan w2v-BERT 2.0 goes from **45.6% WER at 1 h to 32.2% at 10 h and 30.3% at 100 h (Akan, no LM)**, Ewe from 47.9% to 35.4% to 32.2% (Ewe) ([benchmark](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.10968v1)). A 5-gram KenLM helped sharply in some cells (Shona w2v-BERT at 50 h, 23.5% to 8.6%, Shona) and hurt in others (Bemba XLS-R at 20 h, 49.6% to 60.7%, Bemba). Whisper large-v3 becomes competitive near 50 h (Kinyarwanda **12.51% WER at 50 h and 9.82% at 200 h**, Kinyarwanda, which does not mark tone), at 4.5 to 8.9 GPU-hours on one H100 or A100 ([Kinyarwanda study](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.07221v1)).

| ASR option | Licence | What the notes show |
|---|---|---|
| Omnilingual CTC/LLM v2 (300M to 7B) | Apache-2.0 | Igala CER 27.6 (7B LLM); character tokenizer suits diacritics; AfriVox Yoruba WER 31.96% for 7B-CTC (Yoruba, tone-stripped) |
| w2v-BERT 2.0 | Believed MIT, unverified | Best 1 to 10 h generalist; DONDO multilingual Apache-2.0 checkpoints average about 10 to 13% WER on 27 African varieties (religious read speech) ([DONDO](https://huggingface.co/papers/2607.21540.md)) |
| MMS-1B-all | Believed CC-BY-NC, unverified | Used as aligner in DunDun; fails at 1 h on Ewe |
| Whisper large-v3 | Believed MIT, unverified | Zero-shot Yoruba WER 93.77% (Yoruba); strong only with about 50 h or more ([AfriVox](https://aclanthology.org/2026.eacl-long.122.pdf)) |
| Gemini, GPT-4o zero-shot | Commercial | Yoruba WER 27.35% for Gemini-3.0-flash and 101.14% for GPT-4o audio (Yoruba, tone-stripped); no Igala claim or measurement ([AfriVox](https://aclanthology.org/2026.eacl-long.122.pdf)) |

Speech LLMs are not a shortcut. On the NaijaVoices subset, MMS-1b-all beat both Gemini-2.0-flash and GPT-4o on Yoruba (44.05% against 78.46% and 107.25%, Yoruba) and fine-tuning Qwen2.5-Omni on about 280 h per language cut Yoruba WER from 120.84% to 71.29% (Yoruba) ([AfriVox](https://aclanthology.org/2026.eacl-long.122.pdf)). Even with about 600 h of training, MMS-300M reached only 51.65% WER on the NaijaVoices Yoruba test set ([NaijaVoices](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20564)), a realistic prior for how hard tonal Nigerian ASR remains.

Synthetic speech helps only with a real anchor: Hausa with **250 h real plus 250 h synthetic matched 500 h real (Hausa, WER 27.02% against 26.91%)**, while Dholuo was inconsistent and Chichewa gained about 6.5% relative ([DeRenzi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.17578)). LLM correction of ASR output has no direct evidence for a language like Igala: West Frisian shows large gains for GPT-5.1 and almost none for Qwen3, and retrieval of past error pairs helped Persian ([West Frisian GER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.19711), [GEC-RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.10734v1)). The notes found no paper that injects a grammar or lexicon into the prompt for a language the LLM barely knows.

**Inference.** Expect modest, noisy gains below 10 h: Meta already used about 7.6 h and landed at CER 27.6 (Igala), and the Nigerian comparator stayed near 30, so do not promise usable transcripts at 10 h. The cheapest sequence is: baseline Omnilingual on the held-out test, fine-tune CTC 300M and 1B (and w2v-BERT 2.0 at lr 5e-5 for comparison), add a KenLM trained on all Igala text after validating it per language, then test Gemini 3.1 Pro with the grammar prompt and retrieved lexicon on the n-best list, with and without the grammar, against annotator references. Skip Yoruba-then-Igala staged fine-tuning, since Omnilingual already contains 828.1 h of Yoruba and the Hausa-to-Igbo transfer in a Nigerian benchmark was weak ([engrXiv 6650](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/view/6650)). Keep synthetic ASR data until the TTS voice is validated by speakers, and note that one synthetic voice adds no speaker diversity. The 45-minute GRN recording is far too small to train on; use it as a pilot (below).

## Collection, pay and licence: roughly $81 to $1,370 per transcribed hour

Collection recipes diverge by scale, and a six-annotator community can only copy the small ones. The commissioned Omnilingual design asked for **10 h from 10 native speakers per language (across about 350 to 400 languages)**, with open-ended survey-style prompts and linguist spot checks; the most common critical audio problem was pause or silence (27.25% of files) and the most common transcript problem was mismatch (51.18%) ([Omnilingual ASR HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.09690)). WAXAL ASR used image prompts over 50+ topics, a 15 s minimum per clip, one annotator per recording and transcription of only about 10% of the audio (14 languages) ([WAXAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02734v3)). NaijaVoices used read speech from expert-written, double-reviewed sentences, so the prompt is the transcript and no transcription is needed ([NaijaVoices](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20564v1)). Swivuriso (South African languages) had language teams write unscripted prompts in L1 and found scraped scripted text with non-standard orthography inflated error ([Swivuriso](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02201)). Quality gates: Swivuriso records 48 kHz and runs an SNR check at session start; AfriVoices-KE (Kenyan languages) required about 40 dB SNR, rejected 6 to 17% of unscripted clips and found noise caused 30.5% of rejections ([AfriVoices-KE](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08448)); NaijaS2ST used 30 dB at 48 kHz on Telegram, with $15 per 250 utterances in Nigeria ([NaijaS2ST](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.16287)). Esethu found no substantial quality gap between phone and laptop microphones for isiXhosa ([Esethu](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15916v1)).

Transcription is the cost driver. Corrected transcription of a predominantly oral language (Bambara) took about **30 labour-hours per audio hour in the lab and 36 in the field**, and about 17 with ASR pre-transcription ([cost analysis](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.12781v1), [ANV Bambara](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.18557)). The only published rate card is AfriVoices-KE's (Kenya): about **$16 per validated voice hour, $38 per transcription hour, $27 per review hour, $0.08 per generated sentence**, all above Kenyan minimum wage ([AfriVoices-KE](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08448)). Total-cost anchors are $2.2 million for more than 9,000 h in 18 languages across Kenya, Nigeria and South Africa ([Gates Africa](https://x.com/GatesAfrica/status/1994768311985336582)) and a footnote placing NaijaVoices' true cost above $600,000, about $345 per Hausa hour if split equally, an authors' estimate ([CLEAR Global](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17578v1)). One of the notes could not find that footnote in other papers it checked, so treat it as a single-source figure. No Nigeria-specific transcriber rate or studio voice-actor rate was found.

**Inference.** Budget arithmetic, all mine. If the AfriVoices-KE rates apply per audio hour, a transcribed and reviewed spontaneous hour costs $16 + $38 + $27 = **$81** in direct pay; if they apply per working hour with the 17 to 36 labour-hour multiplier, the same hour costs about **$460 to $1,370** (17 x $27 to 36 x $38). The truth depends on local wages and on how much ASR pre-transcription removes. Ten transcribed hours is then $810 to $13,700 before coordination and overhead, and 20 hours is under $30,000 at the upper bound. The 45-minute GRN audio is a cheap calibration: transcribing it with the six annotators yields their own labour-hours per audio hour (12.75 to 27 labour-hours at the Bambara ratios) and a first measurement of tone-mark disagreement. Pay speakers per validated hour near $16 and sentence writers per item (Kenya $0.08, Nigeria translation $0.50 per sentence in NaijaS2ST), and treat studio voice talent as a negotiated line because no source gives a rate. To structure the corpus: about 10 speakers at about 1 h each (Omnilingual's design), a read subset from community-written sentences (no transcription cost, tone-marked tier possible) and a spontaneous subset from image and L1 text prompts that is transcribed with ASR pre-transcription, with the test set drawn only from spontaneous speech, since careful read speech degraded a model trained on conversational data for Uneme (WER 0.997 conversational against 1.622 careful, Uneme) ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04716)).

Consent and licence: Swivuriso and NaijaVoices datasets bar voice cloning and speaker identification, and AfriVoices-KE stores phone numbers and IDs only for payment, with anonymised IDs and encrypted storage ([Swivuriso](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.02201), [NaijaVoices Ibono/Obolo release](https://mozilladatacollective.com/datasets/cmin6i6da001so707uwggcn7a), [AfriVoices-KE](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.08448)). A TTS speaker's written consent must name the synthetic voice and its release terms; the notes did not cover cloning ethics specific to African voices, so this is a gap, not a finding. Licences funders accepted: CC-BY-4.0 or CC-BY-SA-4.0 with partner-retained ownership (WAXAL, 27 languages in the blog, per-partner licences on the card), CC-BY-4.0 with a request form (AfriVoices-KE, Gates-funded), CC-BY-NC-SA-4.0 with a commercial waiver (NaijaVoices, Esethu), and CC0 (Common Voice) ([Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/waxal-a-large-scale-open-resource-for-african-language-speech-technology/), [Esethu](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15916v1)). Esethu adds a community-ownership mechanism: commercial fees waived for African-headquartered entities and proceeds reinvested in further isiXhosa data. The check on the GRN recording: confirm whether the permission covers redistributing audio and transcripts, since the notes do not say. Absent that, keep it as a private pilot.

## A frozen test set and a blind arena beat any single metric

The notes converge on a panel rather than a number. For ASR, no standard normalisation exists: AfriVox strips diacritics before WER (Yoruba, Igbo, Hausa), WAXAL-NET keeps them, so numbers cannot be compared across papers ([AfriVox](https://aclanthology.org/2026.eacl-long.122), [WAXAL-NET](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02375)). A linguistically informed alternative adds Feature Error Rate and a tone-restricted Tone Error Rate, and on Yoruba FLEURS the best model had WER 0.788, CER 0.305 and TER 0.372 with Mid tone worst ([Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.04716)). WAXAL-NET also found reference-text artefacts (truncated references scored as insertions) and used native-speaker audits of 40 clips per language-model pair to catch what the metric missed ([WAXAL-NET](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02375)). For TTS, the evidence is that metrics disagree: listeners and WER ranked OpenBibleTTS systems differently ([OpenBibleTTS](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.09553v1)), and DunDun's reference-free tone metric was calibrated on 126 Yoruba clips and checked on 67 blind A/B trials (89.6% tone-correct picks, Yoruba, 3 listeners) while its authors say it does not resolve trial-by-trial tracking ([DunDun](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.14817v1)).

Arena-style blind A/B testing with Bradley-Terry fits and bootstrap intervals is the established pattern, and A/B tests were reported as more reliable than MOS for comparing systems ([TTS Arena](https://huggingface.co/blog/arena-tts), [Camp et al. 2023](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2023/camp23_interspeech.pdf)). The sizing conventions do not fit six people: CMOS uses 20 or more judges and 50 or more utterances ([NaturalSpeech](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.04421)), and stable MOS significance needed more than 30 listeners ([Wester et al.](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2015/wester15c_interspeech.html)). Small-community precedents are five native evaluators on 50 synthesized utterances per language (Thiomi) and three bilingual annotators per survey (NaijaS2ST) ([Thiomi](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29244), [NaijaS2ST](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.16287)). The Luna-TTS report reached a rankable arena with 1,319 pairwise comparisons over 100 prompts and six systems, and still had overlapping top intervals ([Luna-TTS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11593)). Benchmark authors control leakage with speaker-disjoint splits, separation of old and new test sets, and a hidden online test server ([AfriVox-v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.03590), [ML-SUPERB 2.0](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.07139)); WAXAL's original splits were not speaker-disjoint, as its `_v2` re-partition for six languages implies ([WaxalNLP card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/google/WaxalNLP)).

**Inference.** Design for six annotators by raising items, not judges. Each of the six judges the same 100 pairs (600 judgments per two-system comparison, a number I chose), with clips of 3 to 8 s so both can be replayed, random A/B order, a forced choice plus "tie or both bad" and an optional "word or tone wrong" tag, about 10% duplicate pairs to measure intra-rater consistency, and a paired bootstrap that resamples items, not judgments. Add separate minimal-pair items (same segments, different tone patterns, "which word did you hear") because naturalness preference hides tone errors, and a dictation intelligibility test. Because the six annotators also write transcripts, recruit the roughly 10 recorded speakers as rotating listeners, excluding their own utterances, to add judges and independence. For ASR, the headline is CER and WER on text normalised by lowercasing, punctuation removal, Unicode NFC, tone stripping and the panel's contraction map, with tone-retained scores and a TER-style diagnostic as secondary; CER is the safer headline when word-boundary disagreement is high, as Uneme's CER 0.461 against WER 0.997 (Uneme) suggests. Scoring against the minimum edit distance over two or three accepted spellings is a natural extension, though none of the papers does it. The test set should be speaker-disjoint, sentence-disjoint from every training text, double-annotated and reconciled, tagged by speaker dialect and origin with per-tag scores, and held privately (or released after a delay) because public sentences leak into LLM training. That conflicts with funders' open-benchmark preference, and a tiered release resolves it: open dev set, hidden test. TTS evaluation also needs a validated Igala ASR (DunDun and NaijaS2ST both score through an ASR), which is one more reason to build the ASR baseline first. Report the normalisation with every number.

## No call is open, but Igala sits in a gap every funder names

The September 2026 announcements gave direction without a door. The Gates Foundation and Google press release (18 September) commits "$100 million" for farmers in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia and includes open-source speech and text datasets for more than 40 African languages, delivered through Masakhane Research Foundation and Digital Umuganda ([Gates Foundation](https://www.gatesfoundation.org/ideas/media-center/press-releases/2026/09/google-ai-farmers)). On 21 September a Gates-convened coalition of 60 initial signatories set a five-year goal for AI in underrepresented languages, with four pillars: openly licensed language data, measurement through benchmarks, tools builders can use, and privacy, consent and data-sovereignty safeguards; governance is to be developed over the coming year ([Let's Data Science](https://letsdatascience.com/news/gates-foundation-coalition-targets-ais-language-data-gap-8d851904)). The live calls had already closed: the Masakhane Hub RFP (expressions of interest closed 25 January 2026) and LINGUA Africa, whose data-creation tier offered up to **$50,000 cash plus $50,000 compute credits** and drew more than 800 applications from 64 countries for 26 awards ([HapaKenya](https://hapakenya.com/2026/01/12/masakhane-hub-launches-landmark-funding-initiative-to-scale-ai-for-50-african-languages/), [iAfrica](https://iafrica.com/masakhane-african-languages-hub-joins-microsoft-gates-foundation-and-google-org-to-launch-lingua-africa/), [Brittle Paper](https://brittlepaper.com/2026/08/masakhane-names-26-grantees-for-lingua-africa-its-push-to-put-african-languages-at-the-center-of-ai/)). No next round is stated. LINGUA named community engagement and cross-institutional collaboration as criteria and allowed non-African applicants only with a meaningful Africa-based partner.

What funded work looks like: hours times languages times open licence times a named local partner, then a downstream proof. African Next Voices ($2.2 million, more than 9,000 h, 18 languages), NaijaVoices (Lacuna Fund, 1,838.54 h across Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba), and WAXAL (Google Research lead, partners keep ownership, CC-BY-4.0 with per-partner variants) are the precedents ([Gates Africa](https://x.com/GatesAfrica/status/1994768311985336582), [NaijaVoices](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20564v1), [WAXAL](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02734v3)). Two gaps are visible. The WAXAL paper reports no baselines, and the baselines came in a separate WAXAL-NET paper plus a Zindi challenge (1,462 entrants, Lingala and Shona) ([WAXAL-NET](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.02375), [tech.africa](https://tech.africa/waxal-african-speech-challenge/)). NaijaVoices' split put about 3 h each into dev and test out of 1,838.54 h (Hausa, Igbo, Yoruba), leaving little for evaluation ([NaijaVoices](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.20564v1)). Nothing retrieved states what Google wants from partners in data licensing or benchmark access, and the WAXAL full language list was only readable through the Hugging Face card.

**Inference.** The Gates and Google 40-language line almost certainly routes through the existing Masakhane Hub machinery, so a direct application is unlikely to exist. Practical doors: a subgrant or consortium slot under a Hub awardee, the next Hub or LINGUA round if announced, and the coalition working groups, where a governance window to roughly autumn 2027 lets a community consent model be offered as an example. The proposal should win on what small teams can do and large ones skip: a documented consent and compensation record (AfriVoices-KE's published rate card is a rare precedent), a speaker-disjoint benchmark with baselines, community governance and oral-history provenance, and the existing Gemini 3.1 Pro text model as the language-model tie-in. To Gates, frame it to the four coalition pillars plus a sector use case (LINGUA's list: agriculture, health, education, civic services). To Google, offer a WAXAL-compatible open dev set, a documented protocol and a possible challenge. CC-BY-4.0 with partner-retained ownership is the path of least resistance for both funders, and Esethu-style non-commercial licensing is defensible under the sovereignty pillar but may conflict with the "openly licensed" pillar; the sources do not resolve that tension. Verify the WAXAL language table and the Lanfrica Igala set before claiming any "first".

## Recommended sequence

Order of work is TTS-first for building, but an ASR measurement comes first for dependency reasons: it costs nothing, it validates the evaluation chain for TTS, and it pre-transcribes later recordings. This sequence is my synthesis; each gate is an inference.

| Step | Work | Output | Gate to continue |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Confirm what the GRN permission allows; contact Lanfrica and NaijaVoices about the Igala set; check OmniVoice and EveryVoice licences; run Omnilingual 7B LLM and CTC v2 on the 45-minute recording; transcribe it with the six annotators to measure labour-hours and tone disagreement; test Gemini 3.1 Pro correction on the n-best | Igala baseline CER, labour ratio, tone-agreement rate, licence decisions | Licence for open release settled; baseline measured |
| 1 | Write the orthography and normalisation table, tone convention, consent form (naming synthetic voice release and banning cloning and speaker identification), licence plan; freeze test-set rules | Conventions document | Annotators agree on spelling and tone marking on a pilot sample |
| 2 | TTS pilot: one speaker, 1 to 2 h in a studio box on a tone-marked, domain-matched script; fine-tune OmniVoice (and MMS-TTS if non-commercial release is acceptable) | First voice; arena of 2 or 3 systems | Native listeners find words intelligible; tone minimal-pair accuracy above chance |
| 3 | Scale TTS to 5 h, then up to 15 h while the arena shows gains (Yoruba CER gains shrink between 5 and 15 h); train EveryVoice or VITS as a third entrant | Release candidate voice, tone test, intelligibility dictation, MOS; LLM-restored versus gold-marked input scored separately | Win rate and tone items clear a preset bar; release terms agreed |
| 4 | ASR corpus: about 10 speakers at about 1 h each; read subset plus image and L1-prompted spontaneous subset; ASR pre-transcription; 10% double transcription; SNR and duration gates | 10 h tone-stripped corpus with a tone-marked subset; frozen speaker-disjoint spontaneous test set | Rejection rates and agreement acceptable on a pilot batch |
| 5 | Fine-tune Omnilingual CTC 300M and 1B, compare w2v-BERT 2.0, add a validated KenLM, then test the Gemini grammar and retrieval correction | Baseline table with CER, WER, tone-retained and TER scores, per-dialect | Beat the Igala 27.6 CER baseline on the same normalisation; otherwise report honestly |
| 6 | If funded: grow toward 20 to 50 h; use synthetic speech only after the voice is validated; run the funding approach in parallel from step 0 | Larger corpus, public dev set, hidden test, optional challenge | Funder and partner commitments |

## Conclusion

Two findings change the problem. Igala ASR already exists at roughly 28 CER, so the project's value is a better, tone-aware, community-owned baseline plus TTS and an honest benchmark, not a first corpus. And the evidence base is shallow in exactly the places that matter: every hour-to-quality curve is from Yoruba, Efik, Akan, Ewe or Kinyarwanda, tone behaviour for Igala is undocumented outside non-scholarly sources, and the two most useful TTS claims (5 h suffices; tone saturates in an hour) rest on a 2023 abstract and an unreviewed 2026 preprint. The strongest hedge is to make the first weeks of work produce Igala-specific measurements: a baseline CER on a test set the community controls, labour-hours per audio hour, tone-mark agreement, and a first arena result.

The practical implication is that the evaluation design is the main deliverable and the model is replaceable. A hidden, speaker-disjoint, dialect-tagged test set with stated normalisation and a pairwise arena that includes tone items is what funders reward when volume is out of reach, and it protects the project from the failure the notes document repeatedly: fluent audio with wrong words, and ASR numbers that cannot see tone.
